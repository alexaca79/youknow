# Why "You Know ____ Is a Lot Like ____" Works

## The Game in 30 Seconds

Each round, a player draws two random prompts — a real CSA technical task and a completely unrelated everyday situation — and has to connect them on the spot.

> "You know troubleshooting identity issues is a lot like being a nightclub bouncer."

Then they explain why. The room scores on accuracy, creativity, and delivery.

It's fast, funny, and surprisingly hard to do well — because doing it well requires you to actually understand the technical work.

---

## Why This Is More Than a Party Trick

There is a growing body of research across cognitive science, organizational psychology, and learning theory that explains why an activity like this is unusually effective for technical teams. Below are the key mechanisms and the evidence behind each.

---

### 1. Analogical Reasoning Is How Experts Actually Think

Cognitive scientists have studied analogical reasoning — the ability to see structural similarities between domains — for decades. Dedre Gentner's **structure-mapping theory** (1983) showed that analogy is not a surface trick; it requires identifying the relational structure of a concept and mapping it onto a new domain. This is exactly what the game forces players to do: find the shared tensions, tradeoffs, sequences, or coordination problems between, say, designing a landing zone and planning a wedding.

Research by Gentner, Holyoak, and Kokinov (2001) in *The Analogical Mind* found that people who practice analogical reasoning develop stronger mental models and can transfer knowledge across problem types more effectively. For CSAs — whose job is literally to take deep platform knowledge and apply it to novel customer situations — this is a core professional skill dressed up as a game.

**Key finding:** Dunbar (1997) studied how scientists actually solve problems in labs and found that analogy was the single most common reasoning mechanism in real scientific breakthroughs, more frequent than deduction or hypothesis testing.

---

### 2. Forcing Plain Language Builds Customer-Ready Communication

CSAs don't just need to know things — they need to explain those things to customers ranging from CIOs to junior developers. The "curse of knowledge" (Camerer, Loewenstein & Weber, 1989) is well documented: once you understand something deeply, it becomes harder to explain it simply.

This game imposes a structural constraint — you *must* explain a technical concept through a non-technical metaphor — that directly counteracts the curse of knowledge. Research on **explanatory depth** (Rozenblit & Keil, 2002) shows that people consistently overestimate how well they understand complex systems until they're forced to explain them. The game surfaces those gaps in real time, in a low-stakes setting.

Heath & Heath (2007) in *Made to Stick* synthesized communication research into a framework showing that the most memorable, persuasive explanations use concrete analogies and unexpected comparisons — precisely the skill this game trains.

**Key finding:** Explaining technical concepts through analogy is one of the most effective ways to transfer knowledge to non-experts (Glynn, 1991, *Science Education*). The game is essentially rehearsal for customer conversations.

---

### 3. Improv-Style Games Build Psychological Safety

Google's Project Aristotle (2015) found that the single strongest predictor of high-performing teams was **psychological safety** — the belief that you won't be punished for taking interpersonal risks. Amy Edmondson's research at Harvard (1999, 2019) confirmed this across industries: teams that feel safe to be wrong, be silly, and think out loud outperform teams with more raw talent but less trust.

Improv-based team activities are one of the best-studied interventions for building psychological safety. Research by Kirsten Dunlop and colleagues (2017) found that short improv exercises increased willingness to contribute ideas, reduced fear of judgment, and improved collaboration quality — effects that persisted after the exercises ended.

The "You Know" game creates exactly this dynamic: players take a risk (making a weird comparison in front of peers), the structure makes everyone equally vulnerable, and the humor defuses status differences.

**Key finding:** Psychological safety is built through repeated small moments of interpersonal risk-taking in low-stakes contexts (Edmondson, 2019). A well-run game round is a textbook example.

---

### 4. Retrieval Practice Beats Passive Review

The **testing effect** — the finding that actively retrieving information from memory strengthens retention more than re-reading or reviewing — is one of the most robust results in learning science (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006). When a player draws "tuning cost optimization" and has to explain what that actually involves well enough to compare it to "organizing a garage sale," they are doing forced retrieval of real technical knowledge.

This is far more effective for retention than sitting through a slide deck or reading documentation. Research by Karpicke & Blunt (2011) in *Science* found that retrieval practice produced 50% more long-term retention than concept mapping or elaborative study.

**Key finding:** The game functions as disguised retrieval practice — players don't feel like they're studying, but they are actively pulling technical knowledge from memory and restructuring it.

---

### 5. Humor Improves Retention and Social Bonding

Research on humor in learning (Banas, Dunbar, Rodriguez & Liu, 2011) shows that humor increases attention, reduces anxiety, and improves information retention. Humor that is relevant to the content — not random jokes, but the kind of surprising-yet-apt comparisons this game produces — enhances learning without distracting from it.

From a social bonding perspective, shared laughter triggers endorphin release and increases feelings of group affiliation (Dunbar et al., 2012, *Proceedings of the Royal Society B*). For distributed teams that may only see each other at offsites or on video calls, this matters. The game creates inside references ("remember when Alex compared governance to refereeing a youth soccer match?") that become shared team culture.

**Key finding:** Content-relevant humor improves both recall and group cohesion simultaneously.

---

### 6. Cross-Domain Thinking Drives Innovation

Research on **creative cognition** consistently finds that breakthrough ideas come from connecting knowledge across domains. Mednick's associative theory of creativity (1962) proposed that creative ability is essentially the capacity to make useful connections between remote concepts. The game is a direct exercise in remote association.

More recent work by Kaplan & Vakili (2015) in *Academy of Management Review* found that cognitive diversity — the ability to draw on varied mental models — is a stronger predictor of team innovation than demographic diversity alone. Practicing cross-domain thinking makes individuals more cognitively versatile and teams more capable of novel problem-solving.

For CSAs who need to propose architectures that fit unique customer contexts, this kind of flexible thinking is a genuine professional asset.

**Key finding:** The ability to connect distant concepts is a trainable skill (Wan & Chiu, 2002), and structured exercises like this game are an effective way to train it.

## When To Use It

- **Offsites and team days** — as a warm-up or energy reset between sessions
- **Community calls** — a 10-minute round keeps people engaged and creates a reason to stay on the call
- **New hire onboarding** — newcomers learn technical vocabulary by hearing how peers explain it
- **Workshop warm-ups** — primes the room for creative, non-judgmental thinking
- **End-of-week team meetings** — closes the week with laughter instead of status updates

---

## The Short Version

The game works because it sits at the intersection of several well-researched mechanisms: analogical reasoning, retrieval practice, plain-language communication, psychological safety, social bonding through humor, and cross-domain creative thinking. Each of those is individually supported by strong evidence. Combined in a single 90-second game round, they make "You Know ____ Is a Lot Like ____" one of the highest-value low-effort team activities a CSA team can run.
