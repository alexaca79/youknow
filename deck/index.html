Section 1

The Situation

A story in three acts.

Act 1: The Brilliant CSA

Meet our CSA. She's spent three weeks designing a multi-region landing zone with private endpoints, managed identity, and a governance model that would make a compliance officer weep with joy.

She presents it to the customer's VP of Engineering.

Twelve minutes in, the VP's eyes glaze over.

"So… is this like building a house?"

Act 2: The Team Call

Monday standup. Eight CSAs on camera. One shares a war story about debugging a Fabric lakehouse mirroring issue that took four days.

Three teammates nod knowingly. The other four check Outlook.

Not because they don't care — because the story was told in a language only half the room speaks fluently.

Deep expertise. Shallow connection.

Act 3: The Offsite Icebreaker

Someone suggests "two truths and a lie." Again.

It has nothing to do with the work. It doesn't make anyone better at their job. But hey — at least it's awkward for everyone equally.

What if the team activity actually used
the thing CSAs are best at?

Why This Happens — The Research

The Curse of Knowledge: once you understand something deeply, you lose the ability to imagine not understanding it — making it systematically harder to explain.
Camerer, Loewenstein & Weber (1989). Journal of Political Economy.
The Illusion of Explanatory Depth: people consistently overestimate how well they can explain the systems they work with — until they're asked to do it out loud.
Rozenblit & Keil (2002). Cognitive Science.
Expert Blind Spot: domain experts routinely skip steps and use jargon they no longer notice, creating communication failures with non-experts.
Nathan & Petrosino (2003). Journal of the Learning Sciences.

What Doesn't Fix It

  • More slide decks — passive review doesn't build communication skill
  • Generic icebreakers — fun, but don't exercise technical thinking
  • Trivia nights — reward memorization, punish newcomers
  • Presentation practice — useful, but high-stakes and time-intensive

You need something that is low-stakes, high-frequency,
and rewards real technical understanding.

What if one game could exercise deep knowledge,
customer-ready communication, team connection,
and creative thinking — all at once?

Section 2

The Game

How it works, how it's played, how it's scored.

The Format

"You know [technical thing]
is a lot like [everyday thing]."

Then you have to explain why.

Example

"You know designing a landing zone
is a lot like hosting Thanksgiving dinner."

"Both start with a fight about the layout, involve way too many stakeholders who all think they're in charge, and if you skip the planning, someone ends up at the kids' table running production workloads."

How a Round Works

🎲 Draw two prompts → 📖 Read it aloud → 🤔 30s to think → 🎤 60–90s to explain → ⭐ Room scores

What Makes a Good Answer

  • Explains the process in plain language
  • Maps steps or roles from one side to the other
  • Surfaces risks, tradeoffs, or coordination points
  • Lands on a memorable image people can repeat later

Section 3

Why It Works

Six research-backed reasons this is more than a party trick.

1. Analogical Reasoning

The game forces structure-mapping — identifying the relational structure of a concept and mapping it onto a new domain. This is how experts actually solve novel problems.

Analogy was the single most common reasoning mechanism in real scientific breakthroughs — more than deduction or hypothesis testing.
Dunbar, K. (1997). How Scientists Think. Creative Thought.
People who practice analogical reasoning develop stronger mental models and transfer knowledge more effectively across problem types.
Gentner, Holyoak & Kokinov (2001). The Analogical Mind. MIT Press.

2. Plain-Language Communication

The game structurally forces you to explain technical concepts through non-technical metaphors — directly countering the curse of knowledge.

People consistently overestimate how well they understand complex systems — until forced to explain them.
Rozenblit & Keil (2002). Cognitive Science.
The most memorable, persuasive explanations use concrete analogies and unexpected comparisons.
Heath & Heath (2007). Made to Stick. Random House.

3. Psychological Safety

Everyone takes the same risk: making a weird comparison in front of peers. The humor defuses status differences. The structure makes everyone equally vulnerable.

Psychological safety — the belief you won't be punished for taking interpersonal risks — was the #1 predictor of high-performing teams.
Google Project Aristotle (2015); Edmondson (1999, 2019).
Short improv exercises increased willingness to contribute ideas and reduced fear of judgment — effects that persisted after.
Dunlop et al. (2017).

4. Retrieval Practice

When you draw "tuning cost optimization" and have to explain it well enough to compare it to "organizing a garage sale," you're doing forced retrieval of real technical knowledge.

Retrieval practice produced 50% more long-term retention than concept mapping or elaborative study.
Karpicke & Blunt (2011). Science, 331(6018), 772–775.

Players don't feel like they're studying — but they are.

5. Humor & Social Bonding

Content-relevant humor increases attention, reduces anxiety, and improves retention. Shared laughter creates team culture.

Shared laughter triggers endorphin release and increases feelings of group affiliation.
Dunbar et al. (2012). Proceedings of the Royal Society B.
Humor relevant to content enhances learning without distracting from it.
Banas, Dunbar, Rodriguez & Liu (2011). Communication Education.

6. Cross-Domain Creative Thinking

Breakthrough ideas come from connecting knowledge across domains. The game is a direct exercise in remote association.

Cognitive diversity — drawing on varied mental models — is a stronger predictor of team innovation than demographic diversity alone.
Kaplan & Vakili (2015). Academy of Management Review.
The ability to connect distant concepts is a trainable skill, and structured exercises are effective training.
Wan & Chiu (2002). Journal of Creative Behavior.

When To Use This

  • Offsites — warm-up or energy reset between sessions
  • Community calls — 10-min round keeps the call alive
  • New hire onboarding — learn the vocabulary by hearing peers explain it
  • Workshop warm-ups — primes creative, non-judgmental thinking
  • Friday team meetings — end the week with laughter, not status
