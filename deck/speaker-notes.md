## Slide 2 — The Situation (Section Header)

- "Before I show you the game, let me tell you three quick stories. These are composites, but I bet every one of you has lived at least one of them."

---

## Slide 3 — Act 1: The Brilliant CSA

- Tell this like a story, not a slide read. Pace it out.
- "We've got a CSA — they are incredible. Three weeks deep into a multi-region landing zone. Private endpoints, managed identity, governance model, the whole thing. Technically flawless."
- Pause before the fragment reveals.
- "they presents it to the customer's VP of Engineering. Twelve minutes in, the VP's eyes glaze over."
- Let the punchline land: "So… is this like building a house?"
- The point: the depth was real, but the translation wasn't there. Not because she lacks skill — because she's never had a reason to practice explaining it outside of a high-stakes customer meeting.

---

## Slide 4 — Act 2: The Team Call

- "Monday standup. Eight people on camera. Someone shares a war story about a Fabric lakehouse mirroring issue that ate four days of their life."
- "Three teammates nod — they've been there. The other four are checking Outlook. Not because they're disengaged. Because the story was told in a dialect only half the room speaks."
- Emphasize the tagline: "Deep expertise. Shallow connection."
- The point: we have brilliant people who can't always share what they know in a way that connects across specialties.

---

## Slide 5 — Act 3: The Offsite Icebreaker

- Play this for the laugh. Everyone in the room has done "two truths and a lie" too many times.
- "Someone suggests two truths and a lie. Again. It has nothing to do with the actual work. Doesn't make anyone better at their job. But hey — at least it's equally awkward for everyone."
- Then land the reframe: "What if the team activity actually used the thing CSAs are best at?"

## Slide 6 — The Pattern

- This is the summary of the three stories in a single visual.
- Three boxes: massive depth, rarely translate it, team activities ignore it.
- "Three different scenes, one pattern. We have deep expertise that doesn't get exercised in translation, and our team activities don't tap into it at all."

## Slide 7 — Why This Happens — The Research

- Shift tone here — go from storytelling to "here's why this isn't just an anecdote."
- **Curse of Knowledge:** "Once you understand something deeply, you literally lose the ability to imagine not understanding it. This isn't a character flaw — it's a documented cognitive bias. Camerer, Loewenstein, and Weber proved it in 1989."
- **Illusion of Explanatory Depth:** "We all think we can explain the systems we work with. Rozenblit and Keil showed that people consistently overestimate their explanatory ability — until you actually ask them to do it. Then the gaps show up fast."
- **Expert Blind Spot:** "Nathan and Petrosino found that domain experts routinely skip steps and use jargon they don't even notice anymore. It's not laziness — it's what expertise does to your communication."
- The point: this is a structural problem, not a people problem. It affects every technical team.

---

## Slide 8 — What Doesn't Fix It

- Run through each bullet quickly — the audience will recognize all of these.
- **Slide decks:** "Passive consumption. You don't build communication muscle by watching slides."
- **Generic icebreakers:** "Fun in the moment, but they don't exercise the technical thinking that makes CSAs valuable."
- **Trivia nights:** "Rewards memorization. Punishes newcomers. Doesn't help you explain anything."
- **Presentation practice:** "Actually useful — but high-stakes, time-intensive, and hard to do frequently."
- Land the closer: "What you need is something low-stakes, high-frequency, and that rewards real technical understanding."

## Slide 10 — The Game (Section Header)

## Slide 11 — The Format

- Read the format out loud in a game-show voice: "You know [technical thing] is a lot like [everyday thing]."
- "Two blanks. One from the world of what CSAs actually do. One from everyday life. Your job is to connect them — and make it make sense."

---

## Slide 12 — Example

- Read the full prompt: "You know designing a landing zone is a lot like hosting Thanksgiving dinner."
- Then deliver the sample answer with energy. This is your chance to model what good looks like.
- "Both start with a fight about the layout, involve way too many stakeholders who all think they're in charge, and if you skip the planning, someone ends up at the kids' table running production workloads."
- If people laugh, you've set the tone. If they groan, that works too.

---

## Slide 13 — How a Round Works

- Walk through the five steps quickly. This is mechanics, not storytelling.
- "Draw two prompts. Read it out loud. Thirty seconds to think. Sixty to ninety seconds to explain. Room scores."
- Emphasize: "It's fast. A full round takes under three minutes."

---

## Slide 14 — Scoring

- "Three categories, one to five each. Accuracy — does the analogy actually hold up technically? Creativity — was the connection surprising and clever? Delivery — was it clear, funny, or something people will repeat later?"
- "Max fifteen points per round. Simple enough to score in your head or drop in the chat."

---

## Slide 15 — Game Modes

- Don't read the whole table — pick two or three to highlight.
- **Standard:** "One person per round, five rounds. Great for a team meeting."
- **Battle:** "Two people get the same prompt and both explain. The room votes. This is the offsite crowd-pleaser."
- **Customer-Friendly:** "No acronyms allowed. Bonus point if your explanation would land with a customer exec. This one is sneaky — it's basically comms training disguised as a game."
- Mention Lightning and Team modes briefly: "There's also a 30-second lightning mode and a team mode where small groups collaborate."

---

## Slide 16 — What Makes a Good Answer

- "A strong answer does at least two of these: explains the process in plain English, maps steps or roles from one side to the other, shows risks or tradeoffs, and lands on a memorable image."
- "The best answers are the ones people repeat at lunch the next day."

## Slide 17 — Why It Works (Section Header)

- "Now — you might be thinking, this is a fun game, but why am I looking at a deck about it? Because this isn't just fun. There are six well-researched mechanisms at work here."

---

## Slide 18 — Analogical Reasoning

- "The game forces something called structure-mapping — you have to identify the relational structure of a technical concept and map it onto a completely different domain. This isn't a party trick. Dedre Gentner's work showed this is how experts actually solve novel problems."
- "Kevin Dunbar studied how scientists work in real labs and found that analogy was the single most common reasoning mechanism behind real breakthroughs — more common than deduction or hypothesis testing."
- The point for CSAs: "Your job is to take deep platform knowledge and apply it to novel customer situations. This game is practicing exactly that."

---

## Slide 19 — Plain-Language Communication

- "The game structurally forces you to explain a technical concept through a non-technical metaphor. That directly trains the skill CSAs need most in customer conversations."
- "Rozenblit and Keil showed people overestimate their own explanatory depth until they're forced to actually explain. And Heath and Heath showed that the most persuasive, memorable explanations use concrete analogies — exactly what this game produces."
- If you want to be direct: "Every round of this game is a rehearsal for a customer whiteboard session."

---

## Slide 20 — Psychological Safety

- "Google's Project Aristotle — the biggest study they've ever done on team performance — found psychological safety was the number one predictor. Not talent. Not process. Safety."
- "This game builds it because everyone takes the same risk. You're making a weird comparison in front of your peers. The humor levels the playing field. Improv research by Dunlop and colleagues showed that short exercises like this increase willingness to contribute ideas — and the effects persist after the exercise."

---

## Slide 21 — Retrieval Practice

- "This is the sneaky one. When you draw 'tuning cost optimization' and have to explain it well enough to compare it to 'organizing a garage sale,' you're pulling real technical knowledge from memory and restructuring it. That's forced retrieval."
- "Karpicke and Blunt published in Science that retrieval practice produces fifty percent more long-term retention than concept mapping or elaborative study."
- "Players don't feel like they're studying — but they absolutely are."

---

## Slide 22 — Humor & Social Bonding

- "Content-relevant humor — not random jokes, but the kind of surprising-yet-apt comparisons this game produces — increases attention, reduces anxiety, and improves retention."
- "Dunbar's lab showed that shared laughter triggers endorphin release and increases group affiliation. For distributed teams, this matters. The game creates shared references that become team culture."
- If you have an example from a previous session, drop it here: "Last time we played, someone compared governance to refereeing a youth soccer match. People were still quoting it a week later."

---

## Slide 23 — Cross-Domain Creative Thinking

- "Mednick proposed that creative ability is basically the capacity to make useful connections between remote concepts. This game is a direct exercise in remote association."
- "Kaplan and Vakili found that cognitive diversity — the ability to draw on varied mental models — predicts team innovation more than demographic diversity alone. This game trains that diversity within individuals."
- "And the good news from Wan and Chiu: this skill is trainable. The more you practice connecting distant concepts, the better you get."

## Slide 28 — Let's Play a Round

- Walk through each step live:
  1. Click Spin Pair — read the prompt out loud or hit Say It to let the announcer do it.
  2. Ask for a volunteer. Give them 30 seconds to think.
  3. They explain for 60–90 seconds.
  4. Ask everyone to score in the chat: three numbers (accuracy, creativity, delivery).
  5. If time allows, spin again and get a second volunteer. Head-to-head is more fun.
- Tips:
  - If the volunteer freezes, remind them they can peek at the rationale hint.
  - Encourage the room to react — laughing and groaning both count.
  - If you're remote, have people unmute to react. Silence kills the energy.
